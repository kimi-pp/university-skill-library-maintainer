# Chapter 28: Bloody Legislation Against the Expropriated

## Core Idea
The violent expropriation of peasants from their land created a mass of propertyless vagabonds who could not immediately be absorbed by nascent manufactures. Rather than addressing the social crisis they had created, the capitalist class used state power to criminalize the victims of expropriation, treating forced transformation into vagabonds and paupers as voluntary crime. A brutal system of legislation — whipping, branding, enslavement, and execution — was deployed to terrorize the expropriated into accepting wage labour discipline.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Vagabondage Laws (15th-16th Century)
- **The problem**: Expropriated peasants turned into beggars, robbers, vagabonds by circumstance, not inclination
- **State response**: Bloody legislation against vagabondage throughout Western Europe
- **Ideological function**: Treated victims as voluntary criminals; assumed they could return to old conditions that no longer existed
- **Period**: End of 15th century through entire 16th century
- **Purpose**: Force the expropriated into the discipline of the wage system

### Henry VII's Legislation
- **First English laws**: Began under Henry VII against the newly created proletariat
- **Target**: Those who could not be absorbed by nascent manufactures as fast as they were thrown into the world
- **Assumption**: Workers had "good will" to continue under old feudal conditions
- **Function**: Criminalize the social consequences of primitive accumulation

### Henry VIII: Escalation of Brutality
- **1530 Statute**: Beggars old and unable to work receive beggar's licence; sturdy vagabonds face whipping and imprisonment
- **Punishment**: Tied to cart-tail, whipped until blood streams, forced to swear oath to return to birthplace and "put themselves to labour"
- **27 Henry VIII (1535)**: Second arrest: repeated whipping, half ear sliced off; third offence: execution as hardened criminal and enemy of common weal
- **Irony**: The state that created the conditions punishes the victims

### Edward VI: Legalized Slavery (1547)
- **Slavery for idleness**: Refusal to work means condemnation as slave to the denouncer
- **Master's rights**: Feed on bread and water, weak broth, refuse meat; force any work with whip and chains
- **Absence penalty**: 14 days absent = slavery for life; branded with S on forehead or back
- **Escape penalty**: Three escapes = execution as felon
- **Property status**: Can be sold, bequeathed, hired out as personal chattel or cattle
- **Resistance penalty**: Attempts against masters = execution
- **Child appropriation**: Anyone can take vagabonds' children as apprentices (males to 24, females to 20)
- **Escape from apprenticeship**: Runaways become slaves of masters until age limit; can be put in irons and whipped
- **Identification**: Iron rings around neck, arms, or legs for easy identification
- **Parish slaves**: System continued as "roundsmen" into the 19th century

### Elizabeth I: Continuation and Expansion (1572)
- **Unlicensed beggars**: Above 14 years = severely flogged and branded on left ear
- **Repeat offences**: Over 18 on second offence = execution unless taken into service for 2 years
- **Third offence**: Execution without mercy as felons
- **Related statutes**: 18 Elizabeth, c. 13 (1576) and another of 1597 continued the system
- **Duration**: These statutes legally binding until beginning of 18th century; repealed by 12 Anne, c. 23 (1713)

### James I: Further Repression
- **Definition**: Wandering and begging declared rogue and vagabond status
- **Justices' powers**: Public whipping; first offence = 6 months imprisonment; second = 2 years
- **Prison treatment**: Whipped as much and as often as justices think fit
- **Branding**: Incorrigible and dangerous rogues branded with R on left shoulder and set to hard labour
- **Repeat offence**: Caught begging again = execution without mercy
- **Persistence**: Laws remained until 12 Anne, c. 23 (1713)

### International Parallels
- **France**: By mid-17th century, a "kingdom of vagabonds" (truands) established in Paris
- **Louis XVI's ordinance (July 13, 1777)**: Men 16-60 without means or trade sent to galleys
- **Netherlands**: Statute of Charles V (October 1537) with similar provisions
- **Holland**: First edict of States and Towns (March 10, 1614); "Plakaat" of United Provinces (June 26, 1649)
- **Purpose**: Same pattern — criminalize the victims of capitalist transformation

### The Function of State Violence in Primitive Accumulation
- **Not sufficient**: Concentration of capital at one pole and propertyless masses at another is not enough
- **Not sufficient**: Voluntary sale of labour-power is not enough
- **Education/tradition**: Working class comes to view capitalist conditions as "self-evident laws of Nature"
- **Organization**: Fully developed capitalist production breaks down all resistance
- **Relative surplus population**: Keeps wages in a rut corresponding to capital's needs
- **Economic compulsion**: "Dull compulsion of economic relations" completes subjection
- **Direct force**: Still used exceptionally, but primarily during historical genesis
- **State regulation**: Bourgeoisie uses state power to "regulate" wages, lengthen working day, maintain dependence

### Wage Regulation Legislation
- **Statute of Labourers (1349)**: Passed at urgent instance of House of Commons; first major wage regulation
- **French parallel**: Ordinance of 1350 in France under King John; English and French legislation identical in purport
- **Tariff system**: Fixed wages by law for town and country, piece-work and day-work
- **Hiring requirements**: Agricultural labourers to hire by the year; town ones in "open market"
- **Penalty asymmetry**: Paying higher wages = imprisonment; receiving higher wages = more severe punishment
- **1360 statute**: Increased penalties; authorized masters to extort labour at legal rates by corporal punishment
- **Anti-combination**: All worker combinations, contracts, oaths declared null and void from 14th century to 1825

### Class Character of Wage Legislation
- **No maximum only**: State dictated maximum wages but never minimum
- **16th century worsening**: Money wages rose but not in proportion to money depreciation and price rises
- **Continued repression**: Laws keeping wages down remained with ear-clipping and branding
- **5 Elizabeth, c. 3**: Justices of peace empowered to fix wages and modify them seasonally
- **James I extension**: Regulations extended to weavers, spinners, all categories of workers
- **Manufacturing period**: Capitalism became strong enough that wage regulation became impracticable and unnecessary

### The Decline of Wage Regulation
- **8 George II**: Forbade higher day's wage than 2s. 7½d. for journeymen tailors in/around London (except general mourning)
- **13 George III, c. 68**: Gave regulation of silk-weavers' wages to justices of peace
- **1706 ambiguity**: Required two high court judgments to decide if justices' wage mandates applied to non-agricultural labourers
- **1799 survival**: Act ordered Scotch miners' wages continue to be regulated by Elizabethan statute and two Scotch acts (1661, 1671)
- **Whitbread's proposal (1796)**: In House of Commons, proposed legal minimum wage for agricultural labourers — first time in 400 years
- **Pitt's response**: Opposed minimum wage but confessed workers' condition was "cruel"
- **1813 repeal**: Laws for wage regulation finally repealed as absurd anomaly
- **Reason**: Capitalists regulated factories by private legislation; poor-rates made up agricultural wages to minimum

### Persistence of Class Legislation
- **Contract asymmetry**: Provisions on master-workman contracts allow civil action against contract-breaking masters but criminal action against contract-breaking workmen — still in force in 1873
- **Trades' Union laws**: Barbarous laws against unions fell in 1825 before proletarian pressure
- **Partial repeal**: Only in part; beautiful fragments of old statute vanished only in 1859
- **1871 act**: Pretended to remove last traces by legal recognition of Trades' Unions
- **Simultaneous act (June 29, 1871)**: "Act to amend the criminal law relating to violence, threats, and molestation" re-established former state in new shape
- **Exceptional legislation**: Strike/lock-out means withdrawn from common laws and placed under exceptional penal legislation
- **Interpretation**: Fell to masters themselves as justices of the peace
- **Gladstone's bill (1869)**: Proposed abolition of all exceptional penal legislation against working class; never passed second reading
- **Liberal betrayal**: "Great Liberal party" allied with Tories to turn against proletariat that carried it to power
- **Judicial revival**: English judges applied earlier conspiracy laws to labourers' coalitions
- **Conclusion**: Parliament held position of "permanent Trades' Union of the capitalists against the labourers" for 500 years

### French Bourgeois Revolution
- **Right of association**: Workers had just acquired right under revolution
- **Bourgeois response**: June 14, 1791 decree declared all worker coalitions "an attempt against liberty and the declaration of the rights of man"
- **Punishment**: 500 livres fine + deprivation of active citizen rights for one year
- **Purpose**: Confine class struggle within limits comfortable for capital
- **Survival**: Law outlived revolutions and dynastic changes; struck from Penal Code only recently
- **Chapelier's pretext**: Characteristic bourgeois justification for suppressing worker organization

## Key Concepts
- **Vagabondage Laws**: Brutal legislation criminalizing the expropriated peasantry
- **Primitive Accumulation Violence**: State terror as essential element of capitalist origins
- **Wage Regulation**: State enforcement of maximum (but never minimum) wages
- **Anti-Combination Laws**: Criminalization of worker organization from 14th century to 1825
- **Branding/Punishment**: Physical marking and mutilation as disciplinary tools
- **Slavery for Idleness**: Legal slavery imposed on those refusing wage labour
- **Parish Slaves**: System of roundsmen continuing into 19th century
- **Class Legislation**: Laws serving capitalist class interests against labour

## Key Takeaways
1. The state criminalized the social consequences of primitive accumulation, treating forced vagabondage as voluntary crime
2. Brutal legislation — whipping, branding, enslavement, execution — was used to terrorize the expropriated into wage labour
3. Henry VIII's laws escalated to mutilation (ear-clipping) and execution for repeat vagabondage offences
4. Edward VI's 1547 statute legalized actual slavery for those refusing work, with branding and lifetime servitude
5. These laws persisted for over 250 years, with some elements continuing into the 19th century
6. State power was essential to primitive accumulation, regulating wages downward and lengthening the working day
7. The Statute of Labourers (1349) began the pattern of maximum wage legislation that lasted for nearly 500 years
8. Worker combinations were criminalized from the 14th century until 1825, with anti-union laws persisting in modified forms
9. The state acted as a "permanent Trades' Union of the capitalists against the labourers" for five centuries
10. Even after formal repeal, exceptional penal legislation against workers was re-established in new forms
