# Chapter 29: Genesis of the Capitalist Farmer

## Core Idea
While primitive accumulation created the proletariat by expropriating peasants from the land, the capitalist farmer class emerged through a slower, multi-century evolution. The transformation of bailiffs into tenant farmers, then into capitalist farmers employing wage labour, was accelerated by the agricultural revolution of the late 15th and 16th centuries, which enriched farmers while impoverishing the mass of agricultural workers through usurpation of common lands, depreciation of money, and rising agricultural prices.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Origin Question
- **The puzzle**: "Whence came the capitalists originally?" after documenting the creation of the proletariat
- **Direct creation**: Expropriation of agricultural population directly creates landed proprietors, not industrial capitalists
- **Farmer genesis**: Unlike industrial capitalists, farmer origins can be traced through a slow, centuries-long evolution
- **Serf emancipation**: Serfs and free small proprietors held land under different tenures, emancipated under different economic conditions

### The Evolution of the Bailiff to Farmer
- **Feudal bailiff**: First form of farmer in England; himself a serf; similar to Roman villicus but in more limited sphere
- **14th century transition**: Replaced by farmer whom landlord provided with seed, cattle, and implements
- **Metayer/half-farmer**: Farmer advances part of agricultural stock, landlord the other; divide product by contract
- **Farmer proper**: Emerges when farmer makes his own capital breed by employing wage labourers; pays rent to landlord
- **15th century conditions**: Independent peasants and farm-labourers enriched themselves by their own labour; farmer's circumstances were mediocre

### The Agricultural Revolution's Impact (Late 15th-16th Century)
- **Enrichment mechanism**: Agricultural revolution impoverished mass of agricultural people while enriching the farmer class
- **Common land usurpation**: Allowed farmers to augment cattle stock almost without cost; provided richer manure supply
- **Long-term contracts**: 16th century farm contracts often ran for 99 years
- **Money depreciation**: Progressive fall in value of precious metals (price revolution) brought farmers "golden fruit"
- **Wage depression**: Lowered wages, adding portion to farm profits

### The Price Revolution and Farmer Enrichment
- **Rising prices**: Continuous rise in price of corn, wool, meat, all agricultural produce
- **Money capital swelling**: Agricultural price rises swelled farmer's money capital without any action on his part
- **Rent reduction**: Rent (calculated on old money value) diminished in real terms
- **Dual exploitation**: Farmers grew rich at expense of both their labourers and their landlords
- **Result**: England had a class of capitalist farmers, rich by standards of the time, by end of 16th century

### Comparative Perspective: France
- **Régisseur system**: Feudal stewards/collectors became hommes d'affaires through extortion and cheating
- **Social mobility**: Some régisseurs were noblemen themselves
- **Feudal division**: Great feudal territories divided into 100,000+ small homesteads (fiefs, sub-fiefs)
- **Farmer jurisdiction**: Farmers had rights of jurisdiction over soil dwellers in four grades
- **Petty tyranny**: Oppression of agricultural population under numerous petty tyrants
- **Judicial proliferation**: Monteil notes France once had 160,000 judges where 4,000 tribunals suffice today
- **Rent proportions**: Farms paid 1/12 to 1/5 of product in money or kind

### The Middleman Phenomenon
- **Universal pattern**: "In all spheres of social life the lion's share falls to the middleman"
- **Economic domain**: Financiers, stock-exchange speculators, merchants, shopkeepers skim the cream
- **Civil matters**: Lawyer fleeces clients
- **Politics**: Representative more important than voters; minister more important than sovereign
- **Religion**: God pushed into background by Mediator, who is shoved back by priests as inevitable middlemen
- **Application**: Farmers as middlemen between landlords and agricultural labourers

### Harrison's Observation (16th Century)
- **Rent improvement**: "Although peradventure foure pounds of old rent be improved to fortie, toward the end of his term, if he have not six or seven yeares rent lieng by him, fiftie or a hundred pounds, yet will the farmer thinke his gaines verie small"
- **Farmer psychology**: Even with massive rent increases, farmers considered gains small by contemporary standards

### Stafford's Dialogue on Money Depreciation (1581)
- **Conversations**: Dialogue between knight, husbandman, Master Mercer, Goodman Cooper
- **Winners**: "All those that live by buying and selling" — merchants who buy dear and sell dear
- **Farmers**: "All such as have takings of fearmes in their owne manurance [cultivation] at the old rent" — pay at old rate, sell at new rate
- **Losers**: "All noblemen, gentlemen, and all other that live either by a stinted rent or stypend, or do not manure the ground" — fixed income recipients
- **Farmer advantage**: Pay for land cheaply at old rates, sell agricultural products dearly at new rates

## Key Concepts
- **Primitive Accumulation**: Historical process creating preconditions for capitalism
- **Capitalist Farmer**: Farmer who employs wage labour and accumulates capital
- **Agricultural Revolution**: Transformation of agricultural methods and property relations
- **Metayer System**: Sharecropping system dividing product between farmer and landlord
- **Price Revolution**: 16th century inflation from New World silver
- **Common Land Usurpation**: Seizure of communal lands for private capitalist farming
- **Bailiff**: Feudal estate manager; precursor to capitalist farmer
- **Régisseur**: French feudal steward; evolved into capitalist through extortion

## Key Takeaways
1. Capitalist farmers emerged through a slow evolution from feudal bailiffs to tenant farmers to capitalist employers
2. The agricultural revolution of the late 15th-16th centuries accelerated farmer enrichment
3. Usurpation of common lands provided free cattle stock and manure, boosting farm productivity
4. The price revolution (falling money value) allowed farmers to pay old rents while selling at new high prices
5. Rising agricultural prices swelled farmers' money capital without additional effort
6. By the end of the 16th century, England had a rich class of capitalist farmers
7. French farmers (régisseurs) followed a similar path through extortion and feudal exploitation
8. Farmers acted as middlemen, capturing the lion's share between landlords and labourers
