# Chapter 27: Expropriation of the Agricultural Population From the Land

## Core Idea
The foundation of capitalist production required the violent separation of peasant producers from their land and means of subsistence. In England, this process began in the late 15th century through the enclosure movement, where feudal lords forcibly drove peasants from land they had cultivated for generations, transforming arable land into sheep pastures to profit from the rising wool trade. This created a mass of propertyless proletarians forced to sell their labour-power.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Enclosure Movement as Primitive Accumulation
- **Mechanism**: Forcible eviction of peasants from communal and feudal lands
- **Driver**: Rising wool prices from Flemish manufactures created incentive to convert arable to pasture
- **Result**: "A mass of free proletarians was hurled on the labour market"
- **Scale**: Thousands of small holdings consolidated into large estates; entire villages depopulated
- **Historical period**: Last third of 15th century through 16th century as prelude to capitalist mode

### Feudal Property Relations vs. Capitalist Property
- **Feudal basis**: Peasant proprietors with usufruct rights to common land; lords dependent on number of subjects
- **Transition**: New nobility replaced old; money became "the power of all powers"
- **Capitalist requirement**: "Degraded and almost servile condition of the mass of the people"
- **Legal transformation**: Feudal obligations abolished; private property rights "vindicated" for lords
- **Contradiction**: Capitalist wealth required peasant impoverishment

### State Legislation and Class Struggle
- **Henry VII (1489)**: First enclosure laws attempted to limit depopulation, requiring proportion of arable to pasture
- **Henry VIII (1533)**: Limited sheep ownership to 2,000 per owner; ordered rebuilding of decayed farmsteads
- **150 years of struggle**: Peasant resistance and legislative attempts to stop expropriation were "alike fruitless"
- **Bacon's insight**: Laws failed because capitalist system demanded servile labour conditions, not independent peasantry
- **Failure reason**: "The device of King Henry VII... was profound and admirable" but capitalism required the opposite

### The Reformation's Role in Expropriation
- **Church property**: Catholic church was feudal proprietor of vast English lands
- **Spoliation**: Suppression of monasteries transferred church lands to royal favourites and speculators
- **Consequence**: Hereditary sub-tenants driven out en masse; holdings consolidated
- **Pauperism**: Elizabeth I's exclamation "Pauper ubique jacet" (the poor lie everywhere) after touring England
- **Poor laws**: 1597 poor-rate officially recognized pauperism; became perpetual under Charles I (1604)
- **Long-term effect**: Church property was "the religious bulwark of the traditional conditions of landed property" — its fall made those conditions "no longer tenable"

### The 18th Century: Legalized Theft
- **Parliamentary enclosures**: Law itself became "the instrument of the theft of the people's land"
- **Acts of Enclosure**: Decrees by which landlords granted themselves common land as private property
- **Sir F.M. Eden's admission**: Called for "general Act of Parliament for the enclosure of Commons" — admitting parliamentary coup d'état was necessary
- **Contradiction**: Eden tried to represent communal property as private property of landlords, while simultaneously calling for indemnification of expropriated poor
- **Result**: "Communal lands helped... to swell those large farms, that were called in the 18th century capital farms or merchant farms"

### The Disappearance of the Yeomanry
- **17th century**: Yeomanry (independent peasants) still more numerous than farmers; formed Cromwell's backbone
- **1750**: Yeomanry had disappeared
- **1780s**: Last traces of common land for agricultural labourers vanished
- **Cottage legislation**: Laws required 4 acres of land per cottage; by 19th century, labourers lucky to have a garden
- **Landlord-farmer alliance**: "Landlords and farmers... work here hand in hand. A few acres to the cottage would make the labourers too independent"

### The Stuart Restoration and Legal Usurpation
- **Feudal tenure abolished**: Landed proprietors got rid of all feudal obligations to the State
- **Indemnification**: State was "indemnified" by taxes on the peasantry and masses
- **Modern private property**: Rights "vindicated" for estates to which lords had only feudal title
- **Settlement laws**: Had same effect on English agricultural labourers as Boris Godunov's edict on Russian peasantry
- **"Glorious Revolution" (1688)**: Brought landlord and capitalist appropriators to power with William of Orange

### Crown Land Theft
- **Colossal scale**: State lands stolen "on a colossal scale" after Stuart restoration
- **Methods**: Given away, sold at ridiculous prices, or directly seized
- **No legal etiquette**: All done "without the slightest observation of legal etiquette"
- **Foundation**: Crown lands + church estates form basis of "today princely domains of the English oligarchy"
- **Bourgeois support**: Capitalists favored operation to promote free trade in land and increase supply of free proletarians

### The 18th Century Enclosure Literature
- **Vigorous polemic**: Economic literature debated enclosure of commons
- **Hertfordshire example**: 24 farms of 50-150 acres "melted up into three farms"
- **Northamptonshire/Leicestershire**: Large-scale enclosure turned arable to pasture; ploughed land dropped from 1,500 to 50 acres
- **Depopulation evidence**: "An hundred houses and families have in some open-field villages dwindled to eight or ten"
- **Concentration**: "4 or 5 wealthy graziers to engross a large enclosed lordship which was before in the hands of 20 or 30 farmers"
- **Ruins**: "The ruins of former dwelling-houses, barns, stables, &c., are the sole traces of the former inhabitants"

## Key Concepts
- **Enclosure Movement**: Forcible conversion of communal and feudal land to private property
- **Primitive Accumulation**: Historical process of separating producers from means of production
- **Yeomanry**: Class of independent peasant proprietors; backbone of early capitalist opposition
- **Common Land**: Communal property for pasture, timber, firewood — distinct from state property
- **Capital Farms/Merchant Farms**: Large 18th century farms that employed wage labour
- **Poor Laws**: State recognition of pauperism created by expropriation
- **Settlement Laws**: Legislation binding labourers to specific parishes
- **Depopulation**: Removal of peasant population from land through eviction and consolidation

## Key Takeaways
1. Capitalist production required the forcible expropriation of peasants from land they had cultivated for generations
2. The enclosure movement in England (15th-18th centuries) was the primary mechanism of primitive accumulation
3. Rising wool prices from Flemish manufactures provided the economic incentive for landlords to convert arable to pasture
4. State legislation initially attempted to limit enclosure but ultimately became the instrument of expropriation itself
5. The Reformation enabled massive spoliation of church property, accelerating the creation of a propertyless proletariat
6. By the mid-18th century, the yeomanry had disappeared and communal land rights were extinguished
7. The process created the "free" proletariat necessary for capitalist wage labour while simultaneously immiserating the mass of the population
8. The historical reality was "written in the annals of mankind in letters of blood and fire"
