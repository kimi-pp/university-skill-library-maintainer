# Chapter 12: The Working Period

## Core Idea
The working period is the number of connected working-days required in a branch of industry to manufacture a finished product. Unlike the working-day (daily labour time), the working period may extend over many days for products that require continuous, connected labour-processes. The length of the working period affects turnover velocity and the amount of capital that must be advanced, with longer working periods requiring greater capital investments and tying up capital in productive form for extended durations.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Working Period vs. Working Day
- **Working day**: Length of working time during which labourer must daily spend labour-power
- **Working period**: Number of connected working-days required to manufacture finished product
- **Example comparison**: Cotton spinning-mill vs. locomotive works, both with 10-hour working-days
- **Product nature**: Spinning produces discrete product (yarn) daily/weekly; locomotive manufacture requires 3-month continuous process
- **Repetition difference**: Same labour starts over daily/weekly in spinning; locomotive requires repeated labour-processes over long period

### Product Types and Working Periods
- **Discrete products**: Separate, finished units produced in short periods (yarn, coal)
- **Continuous products**: Require connected, extended labour-processes (ships, buildings, railways)
- **Variations within spheres**: Even within same industry, working periods vary by product scale
- **Examples**:
  - Dwelling house vs. large factory (less vs. more time)
  - Locomotive (3 months) vs. armoured man-of-war (1+ year)
  - Grain (nearly 1 year) vs. big cattle (several years) vs. timber (12-100 years)
  - Country road (few months) vs. railway (years)
  - Ordinary carpet (1 week) vs. Gobelin tapestry (years)
- **Infinite variation**: Time consumed in productive act varies infinitely across products

### Working Period and Turnover Velocity
- **Equal capital assumption**: If spinning-mill and locomotive works employ same capital amount with same ratios and working-day length
- **Turnover difference**: Working period length creates difference in turnover velocity
- **Payment terms**: Assuming both made to order, paid on delivery (eliminating circulation process circumstances)
- **Spinning-mill**: Weekly yarn delivery → weekly recovery of circulating capital and fixed capital wear and tear → immediate circuit renewal
- **Locomotive manufacturer**: Must lay out new capital for wages/raw materials weekly for 3 months → capital tied up → renewal only after 3 months
- **Capital requirement**: Locomotive manufacturer needs 12x more circulating capital

### Working Period Definition
- **Discrete vs. continuous labour**: 100 working-days for locomotive manufacture
- **From labourer perspective**: 100 working-days = 100 consecutive separate 10-hour labour-processes (discontinuous magnitude)
- **From product perspective**: 100 working-days = continuous magnitude, one single connected act of production = working-day of 1,000 hours
- **Working period**: Number of connected working-days required for finished product manufacture
- **Partial products**: Product of each working-day is partial, worked upon from day to day, only receiving finished form at working period end

### Crisis Effects on Different Product Types
- **Discrete products**: Interruption affects only today's production not followed by tomorrow's (yarn, coal)
- **Continuous products**: Not only day's work but entire connected act of production interrupted (ships, buildings, railways)
- **Waste consequences**: If job not continued, means of production and labour already consumed wasted
- **Deterioration**: Even if resumed, deterioration inevitably sets in during interruption

### Fixed Capital and Working Period
- **Value accumulation**: During entire working period, fixed capital value daily transferred to product accumulates in layers
- **Fixed capital independence**: Whether steam-engine transfers value to discrete yarn or continuous locomotive product, renewal period unchanged (e.g., 20 years)
- **Return flow**: Value flows back in small doses (weekly) or larger quantities (quarterly) but renewal only after full lifetime
- **Multiple working periods**: As long as individual period for fixed capital value return is shorter than engine lifetime, engine continues functioning across several working periods

### Circulating Capital and Working Period
- **Labour-power**: Bought for definite week, expended during same week, materialised in product
- **Payment timing**: Must be paid for at end of week
- **Weekly repetition**: Investment in labour-power repeated every week during 3 months
- **Non-settlement**: Weekly expenditure doesn't enable settling previous week's labour purchase
- **Capital requirement**: Must lay out wages for 3 months even if paid weekly (leaving credit aside)
- **Raw/auxiliary materials**: Same pattern — one layer of labour after another piled on product
- **Value transfer**: Not only labour-power value but also surplus-value continually transferred to product
- **Unfinished product**: Cannot circulate until finished form achieved

### Capital Tie-Up During Working Period
- **Prolonged stay in production**: Capital advanced for labour, raw materials, etc. in first week held fast in production sphere for entire 3-month term
- **Incorporation**: Incorporated in nascent, unfinished product, cannot pass into circulation as commodities
- **Continuous addition**: New circulating capital doses must be continually added week after week to preceding amounts
- **Accumulation**: Total of successively advanced additional capital grows with working period length

### Equal Capital, Different Outlays
- **Assumption**: Equal capitals invested in spinning and machine-building with equal proportions, working-days, etc.
- **First week**: Same outlay for both
- **Renewal difference**: Spinner recovers capital weekly and can resume operations; machine-builder cannot until 3 months later
- **Capital magnitude**: Different magnitudes of outlay despite equal daily/weekly capital employment
- **Renewal pattern**: Spinner's capital rapidly renewed, same operation repeated; machine-builder's renewal relatively slow, requiring ever new capital additions
- **Result**: Difference in length of time for capital renewal AND in quantity of capital to be advanced

### Two Key Points
1. **Prolonged production stay**: Capital advanced for first week held in production sphere for entire working period, incorporated in unfinished product
2. **Continuous capital addition**: Working period of 3 months requires continuous addition of new circulating capital doses, increasing total advanced capital

### Advance Term vs. Capital Amount
- **Prolonged term**: Working period prolongation extends advance term
- **Increased amount**: Larger capital amount tied up as productive capital
- **No direct correlation**: In cases treated in next chapter, advance term may prolong without corresponding capital amount increase

## Key Concepts
- **Working Period**: Number of connected working-days for finished product manufacture
- **Working Day**: Daily labour time duration
- **Discrete Products**: Separate, finished units (yarn, coal, carpets)
- **Continuous Products**: Products requiring extended connected processes (locomotives, ships, buildings)
- **Partial Products**: Incomplete products worked on day-to-day
- **Turnover Velocity**: Speed at which capital completes circuit
- **Capital Advance**: Initial capital investment
- **Capital Tie-Up**: Capital held in productive form
- **Fixed Capital Accumulation**: Gradual value transfer from fixed capital to product
- **Circulating Capital Addition**: Continuous addition of new capital during working period

## Key Takeaways
1. Working period = number of connected working-days for finished product; working day = daily labour time
2. Discrete products (yarn) have short working periods; continuous products (locomotives) have long working periods
3. Longer working periods reduce turnover velocity and increase capital advance requirements
4. Working period length varies infinitely across different products and even within same industry
5. Crisis interruptions affect discrete and continuous products differently (partial vs. total loss)
6. Fixed capital value accumulates in product during working period but renewal depends on lifetime
7. Circulating capital must be continuously added during working period, tying up increasing capital
8. Equal daily capital employment can result in different total outlays due to working period differences
9. Prolonged working period both extends advance term and increases total capital amount tied up
10. Working period prolongation affects turnover but may not always require proportional capital increase
