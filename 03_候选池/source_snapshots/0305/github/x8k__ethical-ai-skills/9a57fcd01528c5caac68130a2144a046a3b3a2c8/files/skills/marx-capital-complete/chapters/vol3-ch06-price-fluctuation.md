# Chapter 6: The Effect of Price Fluctuation

## Core Idea

This chapter analyzes how price fluctuations, particularly in raw materials, affect the rate of profit. Marx demonstrates that since p' = s/(c + v), any change in the value of constant capital (c) directly affects the rate of profit, even when surplus-value (s) and variable capital (v) remain unchanged. The chapter has two main parts: first, a theoretical analysis of raw material price fluctuations and their direct effects on profit rates; second, an examination of appreciation, depreciation, release, and tie-up of capital. The chapter includes extensive historical documentation of the Cotton Famine (1861-65) to illustrate how raw material price fluctuations (cotton scarcity during the American Civil War) affected English industry, labourers, and capitalists.

## Frameworks Introduced

### I. Fluctuations in the Price of Raw Materials and Their Direct Effects on the Rate of Profit

#### Analytical Assumptions
- **Constant Surplus-Value Rate**: Analysis assumes no change in rate of surplus-value
- **Pure Form Analysis**: Examines case in isolation, though in reality fluctuations may affect labourer numbers, wage levels
- **Combined Effects**: If machinery improvements and raw material price changes simultaneously affect labourers/wages, combine both effects
- **General Principle**: Variations in constant capital value (through savings or price fluctuations) affect rate of profit by changing C in s'(v/C)

#### Raw Materials Definition
- **Inclusivity**: Includes auxiliary materials (indigo, coal, gas)
- **Machinery Raw Materials**: Iron, wood, leather for machinery construction; machinery price affected by raw material fluctuations
- **Natural Wealth**: Natural wealth in iron, coal, wood presents as natural fertility of capital, determining rate of profit irrespective of wage levels

#### Mathematical Relationship
- **Formula**: p' = s/(c + v)
- **Raw Material Price Fall**: c → c - d → C → C - d → p' = s/(C - d) → rate of profit rises
- **Raw Material Price Rise**: c → c + d → C → C + d → p' = s/(C + d) → rate of profit falls
- **Inverse Proportionality**: Rate of profit rises/falls inversely to raw material price (other conditions equal)

#### Importance of Raw Material Prices
- **Industrial Countries**: Low raw material prices crucial even without sales sphere fluctuations
- **Foreign Trade**: Affects profit rate through raw/auxiliary material prices, independent of wages
- **Historical Misunderstanding**: Economists (Torrens) wrongly explained raw material price influence; classical economists (Ricardo) didn't recognize world trade's influence on profit rate
- **Policy Implication**: Importance of eliminating/reducing customs duties on raw materials; English free-traders' main object

#### Concrete Example: Cotton Industry and Flour
- **Flour Use**: 100,000 power-looms + 250,000 hand-looms consumed 41 million lbs. flour annually to smooth warp
- **Additional Use**: +⅓ for bleaching/other processes
- **Annual Value**: £342,000 for preceding 10 years
- **Corn Tariff Effect**: Higher flour prices cost manufacturers £170,000/year extra; minimum £200,000 for 1837
- **Individual Impact**: One firm's flour price difference = £1,000 annually
- **Capitalist Response**: Manufacturers claimed 10-hour labour sufficient if Corn Laws repealed
- **Historical Irony**: Corn Laws repealed, cotton duties removed, but manufacturers' opposition to Ten Hours' Bill became more violent; ten-hour day became law → general wage reduction attempts

#### Value Transfer Differences
- **Raw/Auxiliary Materials**: Value passes entirely and immediately into product value
- **Fixed Capital**: Value transfers gradually through wear and tear
- **Profit Rate Determination**: Determined by total capital value regardless of consumption in production
- **Market Expansion**: Expansion/contraction inversely proportional to individual commodity price
- **Price-Profit Disproportionality**: Product price doesn't rise/fall proportionally to raw material price → profit rate falls lower/rises higher than if products sold at value

#### Productivity and Raw Material Consumption
- **Labour Productivity Growth**: With development, more raw material absorbs definite labour quantity in given time
- **Raw Material Value Component**: Forms ever-growing component of commodity value proportionally to labour productivity development
- **Depreciation Effect**: Machine/auxiliary material portions of value continually decrease per product unit → raw material portion increases proportionally
- **Counterbalance**: Unless offset by value decrease in raw material from growing productivity in its own production

#### Circulating vs. Fixed Capital
- **Circulating Capital**: Raw/auxiliary materials, semi-finished products must be continually replaced entirely through product sales
- **Fixed Capital**: Only depreciation needs renewal via reserve fund
- **Reproduction Crisis**: Rise in raw material price can curtail/arrest reproduction process if product sales don't suffice to replace all commodity elements
- **Partial Operation**: May make impossible to continue at technical scale, forcing partial machinery operation

#### Waste and Price Fluctuations
- **Direct Proportionality**: Waste expense varies directly with raw material price fluctuations
- **Factory Report Example** (April 1850): Cotton price advance causes loss from waste to increase beyond price advance due to 15% waste in coarse yarn spinning
  - Cotton at 3½d./lb.: Waste loss = ½d./lb.
  - Cotton at 7d./lb.: Waste loss = 1d./lb. (not ¾d. proportionally)
- **Cotton Famine Example** (1861-65): Surat cotton waste = 25% vs. American cotton waste = 12½-15%
  - Surat cotton at 2s./lb.: Waste loss = 6d./lb. (25% of 2s.)
  - American cotton at 5-6d./lb.: Waste loss = ¾d./lb. (not momentous)
- **Compensation Mechanism**: Higher waste price + re-introduction as cotton waste partially compensates for loss difference

### II. Appreciation, Depreciation, Release and Tie-Up of Capital

#### Phenomena Interrelation
- **Full Development Requirement**: Requires credit system and world-market competition (basis/vital element of capitalist production)
- **Presentation Limitation**: These forms belong to eventual continuation of work, beyond current scope
- **General Analysis Possible**: Despite specific form requirement, general nature can be discussed
- **Profit Rate Illusion**: Creates impression that profit amount (identical to surplus-value) can increase/decrease independently of surplus-value quantity/rate movements

#### Definitions and Distinctions
- **Appreciation/Depreciation**: Capital value increase/decrease due to general economic conditions, independent of surplus-labour employment
- **Tie-Up of Capital**: Portions of product value must be reconverted into constant/variable capital elements for production to continue at same scale
- **Release of Capital**: Portion of product value previously required for constant/variable capital reconversion becomes disposable/superfluous for same scale production
- **Revenue vs. Capital Release**: Different from revenue release (reduced consumption costs freeing income for accumulation)

#### Appreciation/Depreciation Types
- **Affects**: Constant capital (fixed/circulating) or variable capital
- **Fixed Capital Elements**: Machinery, buildings, other fixed capital
- **Circulating Capital Elements**: Raw/auxiliary materials, semi-finished products, finished commodities in market, commodities in production process

#### Price Fluctuation Effects on Existing Capital
- **General Rule**: For newly invested capital, profit rate inversely proportional to raw material value
- **Existing Capital Complexity**: Large portion in circulation sphere, large portion in production sphere
- **Three Capital Portions**:
  1. Commodities in market awaiting money conversion
  2. Money on hand awaiting reconversion into production elements
  3. Production sphere: means of production (raw/auxiliary materials, semi-finished products, machinery, fixed capital) + products in process

**Price Rise Effects (e.g., Cotton):**
- **Finished/Semi-Finished Goods**: Value rises (cotton goods, yarn, fabrics manufactured with cheaper cotton)
- **Unprocessed Cotton**: Value rises (in stock, in process)
- **Mechanism**: Retrospectively represents more labour-time → adds more than original value to product
- **Compensation**: Appreciation may compensate or overcompensate for profit rate drop from raw material price rise
- **Limitations**:
  - Available supply volume at origin counteracts price increase
  - Semi-finished/finished goods abundance prevents proportional price rise
- **Timing**: More pronounced when supplies scarce at business year close (after harvest)

**Price Fall Effects:**
- **Inverse**: Commodities in market, products in process, raw material supplies depreciate in value
- **Profit Rate Impact**: Depreciation counteracts profit rate rise from raw material price fall

**Value vs. Price**: Analysis assumes price fluctuations express actual value fluctuations, but same effects apply regardless of cause (credit system, competition, etc.)

**Paradoxical Effects:**
- **Depreciation**: May increase profit rate while causing capital value loss
- **Appreciation**: May decrease profit rate while causing capital value gain

#### Fixed Capital Depreciation
- **Moral Depreciation**: Continual improvements lowering use-value/value of existing machinery/factory buildings
- **Early Stage Effect**: Particularly dire during first period of newly introduced machinery before maturity
- **Rapid Obsolescence**: Machinery becomes antiquated before reproducing own value
- **Capitalist Response**: Flagrant working-time prolongation, alternating day/night shifts to reproduce machinery value faster without high wear/tear figures
- **Failure Consequence**: If short effective period not compensated, moral depreciation causes value loss to product, inability to compete even with hand-labour

**Mature Fixed Capital Depreciation:**
- **Reproduction Cheapening**: Machinery can be reproduced more cheaply due to improved methods
- **Value Transfer**: Not from rapid crowding out, but from cheaper reproduction possibility
- **Bankruptcy Effect**: Large enterprises often don't flourish until passing to new owners who buy cheaply, begin with smaller capital outlay

**Agricultural Example**: Causes raising/lowering product price also raise/lower capital value (capital consists largely of grain, cattle, etc.)

#### Capital Tie-Up and Release
- **Tie-Up Necessity**: Portion of product value must be reconverted into production elements for same-scale continuation
- **Release Condition**: Portion previously required for reconversion becomes disposable/superfluous for same-scale production
- **Turnover Relation**: Depends on turnover number

**Example: 1857-58 Cotton Price Fall:**
- **Cotton Purchase**: Spinners bought large supplies at high prices before crash
- **Price Fall**: Cotton price fell nearly 50% after crash
- **Result**: Tie-up of portion of capital in raw material form; profit rate didn't rise proportionally to price fall due to capital tie-up in cotton stocks

**General Effect**:
- **Reconversion Requirement**: Product value portion must be reconverted into raw material form for production continuation
- **Capital Form**: Exists as money-capital when capital must buy raw material in market instead of possessing it
- **Circulation Obstacle**: If this reconversion delayed (raw material not available at usual source), production process interrupted/contracted
- **Credit Dependence**: Intermediate credit required to continue production

### III. Historical Illustration: The Cotton Famine (1861-1865)

#### Background
- **Cause**: American Civil War (1861-65) disrupted cotton supply to England
- **Cotton Sources**: America (3/4), India, Egypt, Brazil
- **English Dependence**: 1860: 774 million lbs. cotton consumed; 750 million from America, 60 million from East Indies, 4 million from Egypt/Brazil
- **1861 Stats**: 617,082 bales from East Indies (vs. 63,770 in 1860), 15,000 from Egypt, 17,084 from Brazil
- **Supply Gap**: American supply largely cut off; East Indies couldn't fill gap

#### Price and Market Effects
- **1861-62 Cotton Prices**:
  - American: 9d. per lb. (1860: 6d.)
  - East Indian: 9d. (1860: 6d.)
  - Brazilian: 9d. (1860: 7d.)
  - Egyptian: 7½d. (1860: 6d.)
- **1863 Prices**: American cotton sold at 18d.-2s. per lb. in Liverpool
- **Surat Cotton**: Normally 6d.-8d. → 1s. 6d. in 1863
- **Egyptian Cotton**: 1861: 7d. → 1863: 1s. 2d.-1s. 5d.
- **Market Saturation**: Indian markets saturated with English goods; export decrease to Asia/Africa

#### Industry Adaptation
- **Factory Closures**: 1861: 60% factories idle in cotton districts; 1862-63: partial reopening
- **Short-Time Working**: Factories working short time (2-3 days/week)
- **Surat Cotton Substitution**: Indian cotton (Surat) used as substitute
- **Technical Problems**:
  - Surat cotton: Short staple, weak, dirty, required different machinery/preparation
  - Waste: 25% vs. American cotton 12½-15%
  - Extra Cost: Requires extra processes (steeping, washing, oiling, special mixing)
  - Machinery Adaptation: Existing machinery not adapted; new machinery experiments
  - Speed Reduction: Machinery speed reduced to put extra twist in yarn

#### Labourer Impact
- **Unemployment**: Hundreds of thousands unemployed
- **Wage Reductions**:
  - Spinners: 1860 average 18s.-25s./week → 1863: 10s.-18s./week
  - Weavers: 1860: 5s. 7d./loom → 1863: 3s. 4d./loom
  - Loom Reduction: Weavers reduced from 4 looms to 2 looms
  - Fines: Increased from 3d.-6d. on American cotton to 1s.-3s. 6d. on Surat
  - General: Wage reductions of 5-7½% + additional losses from inferior cotton/waste use
- **Public Works**: Government public works programs (canals, roads, street-paving, water-works, reservoirs) provided alternative employment
- **Wage Levels**: Public works paid 4s.-12s./week (sometimes for families of 8)
- **Working Conditions**: Ex-skilled factory workers now labouring in sewers 14-18 feet deep, in mud/water 10-12 inches deep, in chilly humid climate
- **Self-Denial**: "amount of self-denial and consideration the exercise of which is most creditable"

#### Capitalist Response
- **Wage Pressure**: Manufacturers acted with relief committees to keep labourers available
- **Emigration Opposition**: Opposed emigration to retain labour force for recovery
- **Rent Extraction**: Deducted rent from wages even when working short time; owned worker cottages
- **Back Rent Expectation**: Expected to collect back rent owed
- **Public Works Competition**: Public works created labour competition; some mills suspended operations due to scarcity of hands
- **Experimental Mixings**: Constant experimentation with different cotton/waste mixtures
  - Wage fluctuations: Operatives' earnings rose/fell with cotton mixture quality
  - Extreme variation: Sometimes 15% below former earnings, then 50-60% below in following weeks

#### Relief and Support
- **Expenditure**: Nearly £3 million spent by guardians, Central Relief Committee, Mansion House Committee (to March 25, 1863)
- **Public Works Act 1863**: Enabled local authorities to:
  1. Borrow money from Exchequer Loan Commissioners
  2. Facilitate town improvements
  3. Provide work and remunerative wages to unemployed
- **Loans**: £883,700 granted by October 1863
- **Criticism**: Public works = new edition of Ateliers nationaux (1848) but instituted in bourgeoisie interests
- **Supplementation**: Relief supplemented wages when family earnings below unemployment relief level

#### Long-Term Effects
- **1864 Recovery**: Complaints of labour scarcity in some districts (e.g., weavers)
- **Causes**: Low wages from inferior yarn + positive scarcity of work-people
- **Strikes**: Numerous wage disputes/strikes over reduced wages
- **House-Rent**: Rent deducted from wages; cottage values decreased 25-50%; cottages that cost 3s. 6d./week now 2s. 4d./week or less

### IV. Wool Industry Example

#### Price Fluctuations
- **Australian Wool**: 1857: 28d./lb. → end of 1857: 17d.
- **Effect**: Manufacturers bought large supplies at high prices before crash
- **Result**: Profit rate didn't rise proportionally to price fall due to capital tie-up in high-priced wool stocks

### V. General Conclusions on Price Fluctuations

#### Capitalist Perception vs. Reality
- **Appearing Independence**: Creates impression that profit amount can vary independently of surplus-value
- **Reality**: Profit amount identical to surplus-value; variations reflect distribution changes, not surplus-value magnitude changes

#### Economic Climate Changes (1865-1894 Note by Engels)
- **Intensified Competition**: Rapid industry development in civilized countries (America, Germany) intensified world-market competition
- **Productive Forces vs. Exchange Laws**: Rapidly expanding productive forces outgrew capitalist commodity exchange laws
- **Symptoms**:
  1. **New Protectionism**: General mania for protective tariffs, protecting export articles
  2. **Trusts**: Manufacturer trusts regulating production, prices, profits
- **Limitation**: Practicable only in relatively favourable economic climate
- **Crisis Vulnerability**: First storm upsets trusts, proves capitalists unfit for production regulation
- **Function**: Accelerates big fish swallowing little fish

## Key Concepts

- **Raw Material Price Fluctuation**: Changes in prices of raw/auxiliary materials affecting profit rate
- **Inverse Relationship**: Rate of profit varies inversely with raw material prices (other conditions equal)
- **Appreciation/Depreciation**: Capital value increase/decrease from economic conditions
- **Tie-Up of Capital**: Product value portion requiring reconversion into production elements
- **Release of Capital**: Previously required product value portion becoming disposable
- **Moral Depreciation**: Machinery value loss from new improvements before physical wear
- **Waste Variation**: Waste expense varying directly with raw material price fluctuations
- **Circulating Capital**: Raw/auxiliary materials, semi-finished products requiring full replacement
- **Fixed Capital**: Machinery/buildings transferring value gradually through wear

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Direct Effect**: Raw material price fluctuations directly affect profit rate by changing constant capital value in p' = s/(c + v)

2. **Inverse Proportionality**: Rate of profit rises/falls inversely to raw material price changes

3. **Industrial Importance**: Low raw material prices crucial for industrial countries, explaining free trade advocacy

4. **Foreign Trade Impact**: Affects profit rate through raw/auxiliary material prices, independent of wage effects

5. **Appreciation/Depreciation**: Existing capital value changes from price fluctuations may compensate or overcompensate for profit rate effects

6. **Tie-Up/Release**: Price fluctuations affect capital tie-up in inventory, delaying reconversion into production

7. **Moral Depreciation**: Rapid obsolescence from improvements reduces machinery value before physical wear

8. **Waste Amplification**: Waste costs amplify raw material price fluctuation effects (15% waste in coarse yarn spinning)

9. **Historical Illustration**: Cotton Famine demonstrates complex effects of raw material scarcity on industry, labourers, capitalists

10. **Contradictory Development**: Capitalist production simultaneously promotes productive force development and creates crises through price fluctuations
