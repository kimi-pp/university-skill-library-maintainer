# Chapter 3: The Relation of the Rate of Profit to the Rate of Surplus-Value

## Core Idea

This chapter provides a rigorous mathematical analysis of the relationship between the rate of profit (p' = s/C) and the rate of surplus-value (s' = s/v), establishing that p' = s'(v/C). Marx systematically examines all possible cases where the factors s', v, and C vary, demonstrating that the rate of profit depends on two main factors: the rate of surplus-value and the value-composition of capital. Through extensive numerical examples and algebraic manipulation, Marx proves that a rising, falling, or constant rate of profit may correspond to a falling, rising, or constant rate of surplus-value, and vice versa. This comprehensive analysis reveals the complex and often inverse relationships between these two measures of capitalist success.

---

## Frameworks Introduced

### I. Analytical Foundations

#### Assumptions for Analysis
- **Profit-Surplus-Value Equality**: Amount of profit = total surplus-value produced by capital during circulation period
- **Exclusions**: Leaves aside surplus-value division into sub-forms (interest, rent, taxes) and profit appropriation via general rate
- **Mathematical Approach**: Analysis conducted purely in mathematical field with given/ascertainable figures

#### Formula Derivation
- **Definitions**:
  - Total capital C = c + v (constant + variable capital)
  - Surplus-value = s
  - Rate of surplus-value s' = s/v
  - Profit p = s (under assumptions)
  - Rate of profit p' = s/C = s/(c+v)
- **Transformation**: Substituting s = s'v into p' formula:
  - p' = s'v/C = s'(v/(c+v))
- **Proportional Relationship**: p' : s' = v : C
  - Rate of profit related to rate of surplus-value as variable capital is to total capital

#### General Principle
- **p' < s'**: Rate of profit always smaller than rate of surplus-value (since v < C = v + c)
- **Exception**: Practically impossible case where v = C (no constant capital, only wages advanced)

#### Additional Factors
1. **Value of Money**: Assumed constant throughout analysis
2. **Turnover**: Excluded for present analysis (treated in Chapter 4)
   - Formula p' = s'(v/C) strictly correct only for one turnover period
   - Annual correction: Substitute annual rate of surplus-value s'n (n = annual turnovers)
3. **Labour Productivity**: Discussed in Volume I Part IV
   - May exert direct influence on individual capital's rate of profit via extra profit from below-average value production
   - Excluded here: Assumes commodities produced under normal social conditions, sold at values

#### Value-Composition and Productivity
- **Capital Composition**: v:c ratio expresses definite degree of labour productivity
- **Alteration**: Changes in v:c ratio (not from mere value changes in c or wage changes) imply corresponding changes in labour productivity
- **Factor Influence**: Changes in c, v, s imply changes in their determining elements (productivity, working-day, intensity, wages)

### II. The Organic Relationship of Variable Capital

#### Constant vs. Variable Capital in Value Generation
- **Constant Capital**: Important only for value it has; quantity of material irrelevant to value generation
- **Example**: £1,500 constant capital = 1,500 tons iron at £1 or 500 tons at £3; same value, different material quantity
- **Irrelevance**: Rate of profit varies inversely to constant capital value regardless of material use-value ratio

**Variable Capital Distinction**
- **Not Value It Has**: What matters is value as index of total labour set in motion, not labour incorporated in it
- **Total Labour Index**: Variable capital value as mere index of total labour (paid + unpaid) set in motion
- **Inverse Relationship**: Less labour contained in variable capital value → greater the surplus-labour portion → higher rate of surplus-value

#### Numerical Example
- **Assumption**: 10-hour working-day = 10s. = 10M
- **Case 1**: Necessary labour = 5 hours = 5s.; surplus-labour = 5 hours; surplus-value = 5s.
- **Case 2**: Necessary labour = 4 hours = 4s.; surplus-labour = 6 hours; surplus-value = 6s.
- **Conclusion**: As variable capital value ceases to be accurate index of labour quantity, rate of surplus-value changes inversely

### III. Mathematical Analysis of Cases

#### Methodological Approach
- **Separation**: Analyze s'(v/C) by treating factors as constant/variable
- **Three Main Cases**:
  1. s' constant, v/C variable
  2. v/C constant, s' variable
  3. All factors variable
- **Comparison Modes**: Cases can represent successive conditions for one capital or comparisons between independent capitals

**Notation**:
- Capital C with variable component v, rate of surplus-value s'
- Capital C1 with variable component v1, rate of surplus-value s'1
- Rates of profit p' and p'1

#### Case I: s' Constant, v/C Variable

**General Formula**:
- p' = s'(v/C); p'1 = s'(v1/C1)
- Proportion: C1/C = E; v1/v = e
- Then: p'1 = s'(ev/EC)
- Proportional relation: p' : p'1 = (v/C) : (v1/C1) = v : v1 (when reduced to percentages)

**Conclusion**: With same rate of surplus-value, rates of profit are proportional to variable capital portions as percentages of total capitals

**Percentage Convenience**:
- For rate of profit determination (not amount), expressing C = 100 (c + v percentages) is convenient
- **Example**: 15,000C = 12,000c + 3,000v + 3,000s → 100C = 80c + 20v + 20s; s' = 100%, p' = 20%
- **Comparison**: 12,000C = 10,800c + 1,200v + 1,200s → 100C = 90c + 10v + 10s; s' = 100%, p' = 10%

**Percentage Limitation**:
- For changes within same capital, percentages obscure changes
- **Example**: 80c + 20v + 20s → 90c + 10v + 10s: Can't tell if due to v decrease, c increase, or both
- **Analysis Requirement**: Need absolute magnitudes to ascertain change nature

**Subcase 1: s' and C constant, v variable**
- **Condition**: C constant requires c changes by same amount as v, opposite direction
- **Original**: 80c + 20v = 100, s' = constant
- **Change**: v → v ± d requires c → c ∓ d
- **Result**: v change alters s (since s = s'v), producing new rate of profit
- **Proportion**: p' : p'1 = v : v1

**Example**:
- I: 15,000C = 12,000c + 3,000v + 3,000s; s' = 100%, p' = 20%
- II: 15,000C = 13,000c + 2,000v + 2,000s; s' = 100%, p' = 13⅓%
- Proportion: 20% : 13⅓% = 3,000 : 2,000

**v Increase Analysis**:
- **Assumption**: Wages remain unchanged → working-day and intensity unchanged
- **Implication**: v rise signifies more labourers employed
- **Example**: 100c + 20v + 10s → 90c + 30v + 15s (s' = 50% constant)
- **Economic Possibility**: Decreasing labour productivity + constant capital shrinkage (agriculture/extractive industries)
- **Alternative**: Same labourers at higher wages
  - Requires working-day prolongation to maintain s'
  - Example: 20 labourers, 10-hour day → 15-hour day with 50% wage increase
  - Result: 90c + 30v + 15s; s' = 50%, p' = 12½%

**v Decrease Analysis**:
- **Reverse**: 90c + 30v + 15s → 100c + 20v + 10s
- **Modern Industry Case**: Increasing productivity, fewer labourers operating larger means of production
- **Wage Fall Case**: Same labourers at lower wages requires working-day reduction to maintain s'

**Subcase 2: s' constant, v variable, C changes through v variation**
- **Condition**: c remains constant while v changes
- **Example**:
  - I: 100c + 20v + 10s; C = 120, s' = 50%, p' = 8⅓%
  - II: 100c + 30v + 15s; C = 130, s' = 50%, p' = 11 7/13%
- **Modern Industry Context**: Variable capital = small part of total; changes relatively small

**Subcase 3: s' and v constant, C variable through c variation**
- **Condition**: v and s' constant, c changes
- **Example**:
  - I: 100c + 20v + 10s; C = 120, s' = 50%, p' = 8⅓%
  - II: 200c + 20v + 10s; C = 220, s' = 50%, p' = 4 6/11%
- **Proportion**: p' : p'1 = C1 : C (inverse to total capital)

#### Case II: v/C Constant, s' Variable

**Proportional Relationship**:
- p' = s'(v/C); with v/C constant, p' varies directly with s'
- **Example**:
  - I: 80c + 20v + 20s; s' = 100%, p' = 20%
  - II: 80c + 20v + 40s; s' = 200%, p' = 40%

**Subcase 1: v and c constant, s' variable through working-day change**
- **Implication**: Working-day prolongation/shortening alters s while v constant
- **Example**:
  - I: 80c + 20v + 20s; s' = 100%, p' = 20%
  - II: 80c + 20v + 40s; s' = 200%, p' = 40%

**Subcase 2: v, c, s' variable through wage changes**
- **Wage Rise**: v increases, s decreases; s' = s/v may remain same
- **Example**:
  - I: 80c + 20v + 20s; s' = 100%, p' = 20%
  - II: 80c + 30v + 30s; s' = 100%, p' = 30%
- **Proportion**: p' : p'1 = (v s') : (v1 s'1) = s : s1 (since s' = s/v constant)

#### Case III: All Factors Variable

**General Analysis**:
- **Formula**: p' = s'(v/C)
- **Proportion**: p'1 : p' = (s'1 v1 / C1) : (s' v / C)
- **Determinants**: Changes in s', v, C all affect p'

**Five Subcases**:

1. **p' rises or falls in same direction as s' while v/C constant**
   - Example: 80c + 20v + 20s (p' = 20%, s' = 100%) → 80c + 20v + 40s (p' = 40%, s' = 200%)

2. **p' and s' rise or fall in opposite directions while v/C changes in same direction as s' but at slower rate**
   - Example: 80c + 20v + 20s (p' = 20%, s' = 100%) → 90c + 20v + 40s (p' = 33⅓%, s' = 200%)
   - s' rises 100%, v/C falls from 20/100 to 20/110 (≈18%), p' rises

3. **p' and s' rise or fall in opposite directions while v/C changes in same direction as s' but at faster rate**
   - Example: 80c + 20v + 20s (p' = 20%, s' = 100%) → 95c + 20v + 40s (p' = 19 1/19%, s' = 200%)
   - s' rises 100%, v/C falls from 20/100 to 20/115 (≈17.4%), p' falls

4. **p' rises while s' falls, or falls while s' rises, if v/C changes inversely to, and at faster rate than, s'**
   - Example: 80c + 20v + 20s (p' = 20%, s' = 100%) → 90c + 10v + 15s (p' = 15%, s' = 150%)
   - s' rises 50%, v/C falls from 20/100 to 10/100 (50%), p' falls

5. **p' remains constant while s' rises or falls, with v/C changing inversely in exactly same proportion**
   - **Condition**: v/C must change inversely and proportionally to s' to keep p' constant
   - **Example**:
     - I: 80c + 20v + 20s; C = 100, s' = 100%, p' = 20%
     - Wages fall: 80c + 16v + 24s; C = 96, s' = 150%, p' = 25%
     - To maintain p' = 20%: Need 104c + 16v + 24s; C = 120, s' = 150%, p' = 20%
   - **Feasibility**: Requires accidental coincidence of conditions (simultaneous wage fall + productivity change altering capital composition)
   - **Exceptional Case**: Branches using only fixed capital and labour with nature-supplied materials
   - **International Comparison**: Same rate of profit based on different rates of surplus-value between countries

### IV. Summary of Findings

#### Main Factors
- **Rate of Profit Dependence**: Depends on two main factors - rate of surplus-value and value-composition of capital

#### Equality Conditions (p'1 = p')
1. **Same Composition, Same s'**: Per cent composition equal and rates of surplus-value equal
2. **Different Composition, Different s'**: Products of s' × v percentages equal (s'v equal as percentage of total capital)
   - Factors s' and v inversely proportional

#### Inequality Conditions (p'1 ≠ p')
1. **Same Composition, Different s'**: Rates of profit related as rates of surplus-value
2. **Same s', Different Composition**: Rates of profit related as variable capital portions
3. **Different s', Different Composition**: Rates of profit related as products s'v (surplus-value quantities as percentage of total capital)

#### General Conclusion
- **Rising/Falling/Constant p'**: May correspond to falling/rising/constant s'
- **Rising/Falling/Constant s'**: May correspond to falling/rising/constant p'
- **All Combinations Possible**: No fixed correlation between rate of profit and rate of surplus-value movements

## Key Concepts

- **Rate of Profit (p')**: s/C = s/(c+v); surplus-value to total capital ratio
- **Rate of Surplus-Value (s')**: s/v; surplus-value to variable capital ratio
- **Value-Composition of Capital**: c:v ratio; reflects labour productivity degree
- **Organic Composition of Capital**: Value-composition adjusted for productivity changes
- **Proportional Relationship**: p' : s' = v : C
- **Inverse Relationship**: p' varies inversely with c/v ratio (all else equal)
- **Direct Relationship**: p' varies directly with s' (v/C constant)
- **Percentage Composition**: Expressing c and v as percentages of total capital for comparison

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **The Fundamental Relationship**: p' = s'(v/C); rate of profit = rate of surplus-value × (variable capital/total capital)

2. **Always Smaller**: Rate of profit always smaller than rate of surplus-value (v < C = v + c)

3. **Variable Capital's Special Role**: Unlike constant capital (important only for value it has), variable capital's value serves as index of total labour set in motion

4. **Inverse Index Relationship**: Less labour in variable capital value → greater surplus-labour portion → higher rate of surplus-value

5. **Five Mathematical Cases**: Systematic analysis reveals all possible combinations of p' and s' movements

6. **No Fixed Correlation**: Rising/falling/constant rates of profit can correspond to opposite or same movements in rates of surplus-value

7. **Two Main Determinants**: Rate of profit depends on rate of surplus-value AND value-composition of capital

8. **Percentage Convenience**: For comparison between capitals, percentage composition useful; for same capital changes, absolute magnitudes necessary

9. **Exceptional Constancy**: p' can remain constant while s' changes only under very specific conditions requiring exact inverse proportionality

10. **International Differences**: Same rate of profit can be based on different rates of surplus-value between countries
