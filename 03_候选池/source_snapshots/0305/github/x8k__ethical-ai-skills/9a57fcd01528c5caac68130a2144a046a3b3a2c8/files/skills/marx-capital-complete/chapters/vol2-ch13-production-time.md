# Chapter 13: The Time of Production

## Core Idea
Production time is always working time (time during which capital is held in production sphere), but not all time capital is in production is necessarily working time. Production time exceeds working time when the subject of labour undergoes natural processes requiring physical, chemical, or physiological changes during which the labour-process is entirely or partially suspended. This creates a divergence between production period and working period that affects turnover velocity and capital requirements.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Production Time vs. Working Time
- **Relationship**: Working time is always production time, but production time can exceed working time
- **Focus**: Not interruptions from labour-power limitations but interruptions independent of labour-process length
- **Natural processes**: Interruptions brought about by product nature and fabrication requirements
- **Subject of labour**: Undergoes natural processes, must change physically/chemically/physiologically
- **Labour suspension**: Labour-process entirely or partially suspended during these periods

### Examples of Production Time Exceeding Working Time
- **Wine production**: Grapes after pressing must ferment, then rest to reach perfection
- **Pottery**: Product must pass through drying process
- **Chemical processes**: Products exposed to conditions to change chemical properties (bleaching)
- **Agriculture**: Winter grain needs ~9 months to mature; labour-process almost entirely suspended between sowing and harvesting
- **Timber**: After sowing and preliminary work, seed requires ~100 years to transform into finished product with minimal labour action
- **Characteristic**: Additional labour drawn on only occasionally during large portion of production time

### Capital Addition Pattern
- **Interval variation**: Condition where additional capital/labour must be supplied to capital already tied up in production obtains with longer/shorter intervals
- **Contrast with Chapter 12**: Working period (Chapter 12) requires continuous capital addition; production time allows longer intervals

### Production Period Structure
- **Two periods**: Production time consists of:
  1. Period during which capital is engaged in labour-process
  2. Period during which capital's form of existence (unfinished product) is abandoned to natural processes without being in labour-process
- **Non-coincidence**: Working period and production period do not coincide; production period longer than working period
- **Turnover effect**: Length of turnover period increases in proportion to production time exceeding working time

### Artificial Reduction of Production Time
- **Technological improvement**: Production time not fixed by natural laws can be shortened artificially
- **Chemical bleaching**: Replaces bleaching on green, more efficient drying apparatus
- **Tanning**: Old method took 6-18 months; new air-pump method takes 1.5-2 months
- **Iron/steel manufacture**: Most magnificent illustration over last 100 years (puddling process to Bessemer process to latest methods)
- **Trade-off**: Time of production reduced tremendously but fixed capital investment increased proportionally

### Shoe-Last Manufacturing Example
- **Unproductive costs**: Considerable portion arises from holding timber 18+ months before dry enough to work
- **No labour-process**: Wood does not pass through any labour-process during drying
- **Turnover determination**: Period of turnover not determined solely by manufacture time but also by unproductive drying time
- **Differential turnover**: Shows times of turnover of different parts of aggregate circulating capital may differ due to production process conditions, not circulation sphere conditions

### Agriculture: Maximum Divergence
- **Seasonal dependence**: In moderate climates, land bears grain once a year; production period ~9 months for winter grain
- **Uncontrollable**: Shortening/lengthening depends on good/bad seasons, cannot be accurately determined/controlled beforehand
- **By-products**: Milk, cheese can be produced/sold in short periods steadily
- **Working time data (Germany)**: Spring 50-60 days, summer 65-80 days, autumn 55-75 days; winter only jobs market goods/building materials
- **Climate effects**: More unfavourable climate = more congested working period, shorter time for capital/labour expenditure

### Russia: Agricultural Example
- **Limited working period**: Northern districts allow field labour only 130-150 days/year
- **Population impact**: 50 out of 65 million European population would be without work 6-8 months if only field labour available
- **Domestic industries**: Local domestic industries developed in villages: weaving, tanning, shoemaking, locksmithing, cutlery
- **Gubernias**: Moscow, Vladimir, Kaluga, Kostroma, Petersburg have generations of peasant artisans
- **Capitalist penetration**: Domestic industry pressed into capitalist production service; merchants supply warp/woof directly or through middlemen
- **Capitalist development**: When capitalist production separates manufacture from agriculture, rural labourer becomes dependent on casual accessory employment, condition deteriorates
- **Capital vs. labour**: For capital, turnover differences evened out; not so for labourer

### Industry vs. Agriculture Patterns
- **Most branches**: Operations proceed evenly year-round; working time same annually; capital outlay passes daily into circulation uniformly
- **Circulating capital return**: Evenly distributed throughout year (market conditions unchanged)
- **Uneven outlay**: In investments where working time = part of production time, return occurs in bulk at natural fixed times
- **Capital advancement**: Must be advanced in larger quantities at a time and for longer periods than enterprises with continuous working periods
- **Fixed capital life**: Considerably greater difference between fixed capital life and time it functions productively

### Fixed Capital During Production Time
- **Interruption**: Difference between working time and production time causes fixed capital employment to be continually interrupted
- **Agriculture example**: Working cattle, implements, machines idle for longer/shorter periods
- **Continuing expenses**: Draught animals require same/near same expenditure for feed during non-work as work periods
- **Dead stock**: Non-use brings depreciation; value transfer calculated according to depreciation time, not functioning time
- **Product pricing**: Product price increases generally since value transfer not calculated by functioning time but by depreciation time
- **Normal conditions**: Idling of fixed capital (with/without current expenses) forms condition of normal employment, like cotton loss in spinning
- **Unproductive expenditure**: Labour-power expended unproductively but unavoidably under normal technical conditions counts as productive
- **Improvement effect**: Reducing unproductive expenditure of instruments, raw material, labour-power reduces product value

### Agriculture: Combined Factors
- **Long working period**: Agriculture combines both longer working period and great difference between working time and production time
- **Hodgskin's observation**: "Difference of time required to complete products of agriculture and other labour species" is main cause of great... (Hodgskin's point about agricultural distinction)

## Key Concepts
- **Production Time**: Total time capital in production sphere
- **Working Time**: Time labour actually expended
- **Working Period**: Number of connected working-days for finished product
- **Production Period**: Working period + time for natural processes
- **Natural Processes**: Physical/chemical/physiological changes requiring time
- **Labour Suspension**: Periods where labour-process paused but product developing
- **Turnover Period**: Time for capital circuit completion
- **Artificial Reduction**: Technological shortening of natural process times
- **Unproductive Costs**: Expenses not directly contributing to production
- **Seasonal Variation**: Natural cycles affecting production timing

## Key Takeaways
1. Production time always includes working time, but can exceed it due to natural processes
2. Natural processes (fermentation, drying, maturing, chemical changes) require time without labour
3. Production time = working period + natural process time; production period longer than working period
4. Longer production time increases turnover period proportionally
5. Artificial reductions (technology) can shorten production times not fixed by natural laws
6. Shoe-last example: 18-month drying time before manufacturing can begin affects turnover
7. Agriculture shows maximum divergence: 9-month production period with short working periods
8. Climate affects working period length and production time divergence
9. Russia's example shows how domestic industries develop to fill non-working periods
10. Fixed capital idling during production time affects pricing (calculated by depreciation, not use)
