# Relvor Market Stress Test

This skill stress-tests any product Surdma builds under Relvor LTD against what the Nigerian market will actually do. It is not a validation tool — it is a survival filter.

The average Nigerian startup fails in its first two years. Most don't die from bad ideas. They die from correct ideas executed against wrong assumptions about how Nigerians buy, trust, pay, and adopt new tools. This skill closes that gap using real data, documented failure patterns, and the pattern recognition of an experienced founder who has watched this market long enough to know what the research cannot tell you.

**This skill is product-agnostic.** It applies to Leja, and to every product Surdma builds after Leja. Trigger it fresh for every new product — do not assume what worked for one product applies to another.

Load both reference files:
- `references/nigeria_market_data.md` — Economy, digital adoption statistics, consumer behavior research (2024–2025)
- `references/founder_pattern_library.md` — 30 pattern-recognition rules a 10–15 year experienced Nigerian founder/CEO would know instinctively

Read them. Then proceed.

## 1. What This Skill Produces

For every product or business decision brought to it, the stress test generates:

**A. The Reality Frame** — What is actually true about this market right now, backed by data  
**B. The Adoption Likelihood Score** — How likely Nigerian users/businesses will actually adopt this, and why  
**C. The Revenue Reality Check** — What the economics actually look like using real Nigerian market data  
**D. The 10–15 Year Founder Lens** — What an experienced Nigerian founder would tell Surdma that no research document will say  
**E. The Kill Conditions** — The specific things that will kill this product in the Nigerian market if ignored  
**F. The One Next Action** — Not a plan. One thing. This week.

---

## 2. The Stress Test Protocol

Run each section in order. Do not skip. Do not compress.

---

### Section A: Reality Frame

**What to establish:**

1. **Market size — bottom-up only**
   - How many people/businesses of this specific type exist in Nigeria right now?
   - Source: NBS data, industry association records, or documented estimates — not global reports extrapolated down
   - What are they currently doing or paying to solve this problem?
   - What percentage of them are reachable in Year 1 with no marketing budget and no brand?

2. **GDP and income context**
   - Nigeria nominal GDP (2024 rebased): ~₦372.8 trillion (~$243B USD)
   - Real GDP growth: 3.38% in 2024, 3.13% YoY Q1 2025; projected ~4.2% in 2025
   - Per capita GDP: ~$1,100 USD (~₦1.65M) — low purchasing power baseline
   - Inflation (2024): 34.8% — purchasing power eroding fast; customers feel poorer even if employed
   - What does this mean for this specific product's price point and willingness to pay?

3. **Digital economy baseline**
   - Digital economy contributed 14.19% to Nigeria's real GDP in Q1 2025 (₦7 trillion of ₦49.34T)
   - ICT sector (telecom-led) = 10.59%; Financial institutions = 3.60%
   - 163 million internet users as of March 2024; 43.5% broadband penetration
   - Nigeria has Africa's largest mobile market: 157 million subscribers
   - Digital transformation market: $11.71B in 2025, growing at 18.17% CAGR to $26.98B by 2030
   - FDI into digital economy: $191M in Q1 2024 (900% increase from $22M in Q1 2023)
   - Apply all of this to the specific product: does it sit in a tailwind or a headwind sector?

4. **The infrastructure reality for this product**
   - What does this product need to function consistently? (internet, power, banking access, smartphone)
   - How often does the Nigerian environment fail to provide that?
   - What happens to the user's experience — and to the product's revenue — when it fails?

---

### Section B: Adoption Likelihood Score

Rate the product from 1–10 on each of the five adoption dimensions. Be specific. Cite evidence from the reference files or documented market behavior. Do not score from intuition alone.

**Dimension 1: Trust Barrier (1 = very high barrier, 10 = very low barrier)**
- Nigerian consumer trust in new digital products is structurally low. Trust, security, and privacy are the primary drivers of digital product adoption (M-Payment Adoption Model, multiple studies 2024–2025).
- Key questions: Does the customer have to give you money, data, or operational control before they've experienced value? If yes: trust barrier is high.
- Evidence of trust transfer (what makes a low-trust market trust a new product): word-of-mouth from known peers, physical proof artifacts (receipts, documents), brand association with a trusted institution, visible regulatory compliance, cash-first option before digital.
- Note: Over 65% of Nigerians distrust digital payments for large transactions (NBS 2024). This is not a technology problem — it is a relationship problem. Products that solve it with design rather than promises win.

**Dimension 2: Distribution Realism (1 = nearly impossible, 10 = natural fit)**
- How does this product actually reach its first 10 paying customers?
- Nigerian distribution reality: WhatsApp groups are primary discovery for SME tools; Instagram and WhatsApp are dominant for B2C; word-of-mouth within communities is more reliable than paid acquisition
- Nigerian e-commerce consumers prefer social commerce (WhatsApp, Instagram) over platform commerce — "they don't need to log in somewhere new" (documented behavior, 2025)
- For B2B: industry association chapters (e.g., NIESV, trade unions, professional groups) are the fastest trust-validated channel
- Does this product have a natural distribution channel that already exists, or does it need to build one from scratch?

**Dimension 3: Willingness to Pay (1 = price-sensitive to the point of rejection, 10 = obvious value / easy sale)**
- Nigerian SMEs are 90% of all businesses; they account for 80–90% of C2B payments
- SME digital literacy is low: 67% of Southeast Nigeria SMEs cite gaps in digital literacy; 54% cite poor internet infrastructure as major barriers (NCC 2024)
- SMEs with higher digital adoption report 15% revenue growth vs. below 5% for low adoption (2025 research, 200 SMEs studied)
- BUT: adoption cost is the #2 blocker to digital adoption for Nigerian SMEs (after digital culture)
- The pricing rule for Nigeria: if the monthly subscription exceeds 5–10% of the expected monthly revenue the tool generates for a small business, rejection is near-certain
- Transaction-based or pay-per-use pricing reliably outperforms subscription pricing in the Nigerian SME context
- Cash-on-delivery preference persists across segments: even digital-native consumers prefer to inspect before paying

**Dimension 4: Infrastructure Dependency (1 = product collapses without reliable infrastructure, 10 = survives power and internet outages)**
- Nigeria: load shedding, intermittent data, and inconsistent network quality are structural, not exceptions
- Products that degrade gracefully (offline mode, WhatsApp fallback, SMS notification) have significantly higher retention than products that require constant connectivity
- Rate how badly this product breaks when the power goes out or data runs out

**Dimension 5: Behavior Change Required (1 = requires complete new behavior, 10 = fits into existing habits)**
- The most dangerous assumption in Nigerian product building: "Nigerians will change how they work once they see the value." They will not change first. They will adopt only when the product fits close enough to what they already do.
- WhatsApp is the operating system of Nigerian SME communication. Products that work with WhatsApp, generate WhatsApp-ready outputs, or replace WhatsApp workflows with something better-but-familiar have much higher adoption rates than products that ask users to leave WhatsApp.
- Rate how much behavioral change this product requires, and from whom

**Adoption Likelihood Score: total out of 50. Interpret:**
- 40–50: Strong adoption potential in Nigeria. Proceed with urgency.
- 30–39: Conditional adoption. Specific friction points must be solved before launch.
- 20–29: High risk. Significant redesign needed for the Nigerian market.
- Below 20: Very high risk. This product may be right for a different market or a later time in Nigeria's development.

### Section C: Revenue Reality Check

**Always use Naira first. USD second.**

Run this calculation for every product:

1. **Unit economics (Year 1)**
   - What is the minimum viable price in Naira that the target customer will pay without requiring a sales pitch?
   - What does it cost to serve one customer per month? (infrastructure, support, AI calls, payment processing)
   - What is the gross margin per paying customer?
   - How many paying customers are needed to cover basic operational costs?

2. **Customer acquisition reality**
   - What is the realistic CAC with zero paid marketing? (community outreach, direct sales, referral)
   - What is the LTV over 24 months?
   - LTV:CAC ratio target: minimum 3:1 in Year 1; 5:1 or higher to be fundable without needing perfect execution

3. **Nigerian-specific revenue risks**
   - Naira depreciation: Nigerian startups raised in USD but earn in Naira face asymmetric cost/revenue pressure. Any product with USD-denominated costs and Naira revenue must show the math survives a 30–50% Naira devaluation event.
   - Nigerian startup funding environment: raised $589M in 2024 (down from $1.2B peak in 2022); H1 2025 saw $163M across Nigeria, down 46%. Early-stage startups without unit economics are not getting funded.
   - Inflation erosion: at 34.8% inflation (2024), a product priced at ₦10,000/month is effectively cheaper every 3 months. This is a pricing tailwind — but it also means costs rise faster than prices if not managed.
   - Single revenue stream risk: 70% of startups relying on a single revenue stream fail (global research). In Nigeria, this risk is amplified by macro volatility. Every product must have a primary and a secondary revenue stream from launch.

4. **Revenue model fit for Nigeria**
   - Transaction-based beats subscription for price-sensitive segments
   - Freemium with a clear upgrade trigger beats free trials with expiry dates
   - B2B beats B2C for early revenue — businesses have a commercial reason to pay; consumers need to feel value before paying
   - "We'll monetize later" is a death sentence in Nigerian market conditions where funding is scarce and inflation is 34.8%

### Section D: The 10–15 Year Founder Lens

This is the most important section. It is not backed by research papers. It is backed by the accumulated pattern recognition of founders who have survived (and failed) in Nigeria long enough to have seen these patterns repeat.

**Read `references/founder_pattern_library.md` before writing this section.**

For every product, identify the top 3–5 patterns from the library that apply. State each pattern, what it means for this specific product, and what the founder must do differently because of it.

These are not warnings. They are decisions that must be made before the market makes them for you.

---

### Section E: Kill Conditions

**What specifically kills this in the Nigerian market if ignored?**

State maximum 5 kill conditions. Each must be:
- Specific (not "trust issues" — "agents will not give you their client payment data until they have seen you handle one transaction correctly, and even then only if you are introduced by someone they already trust")
- Backed by evidence (reference a documented failure, a market behavior pattern, or a specific economic constraint)
- Actionable (what must be done to defuse it, and by when)

Common Nigerian startup kill conditions by category:

**Trust kills:**
- Product asks for money or sensitive data before delivering any value → adoption fails at first contact
- No visible compliance marker (registered company number, regulatory citation, data protection policy) → SME buyers reject at consideration stage

**Distribution kills:**
- No warm introduction into the first community of customers → cold outreach CAC is unaffordable at zero budget
- Product requires customers to change their WhatsApp behavior → retention collapses

**Revenue kills:**
- Monthly subscription above the psychological pain threshold for the target customer → conversion rate too low to sustain the business
- USD-denominated costs against Naira revenue without a hedge → cost inflation kills margins before revenue scales

**Model kills:**
- Two-sided marketplace without solving supply first → liquidity problem that self-reinforces
- Consumer app where monetization is deferred → cash runs out before habit is formed

**Regulatory kills:**
- Product touches payments without understanding CBN's evolving framework → operational disruption risk
- Product stores personal data without NDPC compliance → reputational and legal risk
- Product operates in a sector with pending regulation (e.g., crypto, health data, credit scoring) → regulatory action can zero the product overnight

---

### Section F: One Next Action

After the full stress test, end with exactly one next action. Not a list. Not a plan. The single most important thing Surdma can do this week to move this product closer to reality or to a confident decision to not build it.

Format:
> **This week:** [specific action] → [what you will learn from doing it] → [what decision that enables]

## 3. How to Handle Products That Are Not Leja

This skill is intentionally product-agnostic. When a new product arrives:

1. Do not assume the Nigerian market dynamics from Leja apply. Each product has a different customer, different trust barrier, different distribution reality.
2. Run the full stress test from scratch. Do not abbreviate because a previous product was analyzed.
3. Check whether the new product conflicts with, complements, or is independent of Leja. If it competes with Leja for founder attention before Leja has revenue, flag it as a distraction — once.
4. If the product is clearly out of scope for Relvor's current mission (building Leja to revenue), say so in one sentence and return focus. Do not elaborate the distraction.

## 4. West Africa and Africa as Benchmarks (When Relevant)

When the product's potential market extends beyond Nigeria, add an Africa benchmark section:

**West Africa context:**
- Nigeria = ~50% of West Africa's population (220M of ~440M)
- Nigeria + Ghana + Côte d'Ivoire = ~65% of ECOWAS GDP
- Digital adoption is faster in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire at a consumer level, but Nigeria's volume is unmatched
- Payment interoperability across ECOWAS is improving but not yet reliable — products that assume cross-border payment rails work smoothly are wrong in 2025

**Africa benchmark:**
- Nigeria had 3,360+ startups in 2024 — most in Africa
- African tech funding: $1.42B in H1 2025 (243 deals), up from trough but down significantly from 2022 peak ($2.4B full year)
- Africa continental scale is the vision; Nigerian market validation is the prerequisite

Do not use Africa-scale numbers to justify a product that cannot survive Nigeria first. The Africa market is not a backup plan for a Nigeria product that doesn't work.

## 6. Quality Standard

Every stress test must meet this bar:

- Every market claim is backed by a specific number, a documented source, or a named company case
- No claim is generic ("Nigerian consumers are price-sensitive" without a specific number attached)
- The 10–15 Year Founder Lens contains at least one thing Surdma has not considered
- The kill conditions are specific enough that Surdma can take an action against each one
- The next action is executable this week with zero budget if necessary

If the stress test cannot meet this bar because the product idea is too vague, ask one clarifying question — then proceed with the best available interpretation.
